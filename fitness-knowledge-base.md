# FitHeal Fitness Knowledge Base

A unified reference covering training principles, body recomposition, two complete workout programs, functional anatomy, exercise libraries, and progress-tracking tools. Used by the FitHeal coach to answer user fitness questions.

---

## PART 1 — TRAINING PRINCIPLES

### 1.1 Why Training Is the Driving Force

Weight training is the engine of body recomposition. Nutrition is the fuel; sleep and stress management are the maintenance. Without training, no appreciable muscle is built regardless of how dialed-in everything else is.

Key variables that shape a program: goal, experience level, preferences, schedule, equipment, and injury history. Once known, specific recommendations can be made for **volume, exercise selection, effort, rep range, frequency, and split**.

---

### 1.2 Training Experience Levels

**Beginner (0–2 years):** The body is most primed for muscle growth. Three conditions are enough:
1. Caloric intake not too high or too low.
2. Adequate protein.
3. Weight training with progressive overload.

**Intermediate (~2–5 years) / Advanced (5+ years):** Closer to genetic potential — fine-tuning more variables becomes increasingly important.

---

### 1.3 Four Conditions Especially Favorable for Recomposition

1. **New lifters** — body is maximally primed; stored fat fuels rapid muscle gain.
2. **Detrained lifters** — "muscle memory" allows rapid re-acquisition.
3. **Obese individuals** — large fat reserves fuel muscle building in a deficit.
4. **Anabolic steroid users** — drug-assisted physiology enables simultaneous gain and loss.

---

### 1.4 Three Ways Recomposition Shows Up

**Outcome 1 — Build muscle + lose fat simultaneously.** Classic case, most common in favorable conditions.

**Outcome 2 — Build muscle, maintain fat mass.** Fat mass unchanged but body-fat % drops as lean mass rises. Example: 180 lb at 20% BF → add 10 lb muscle, no fat lost → 190 lb, 36 lb fat → 18.9% BF.

**Outcome 3 — Lose fat, maintain muscle.** BF% improves even without new muscle. Most common in highly advanced trainees. Example: 180 lb at 20% BF → lose 5 lb fat → 175 lb, 31 lb fat → 17.7% BF.

---

### 1.5 Progressive Overload

The gradual increase in stress placed on the body over time. Without greater stress, muscle has no reason to grow.

**Five methods:**
1. Increase load (add weight).
2. Increase reps with the same load.
3. Increase sets.
4. Improve form / technique.
5. Increase rep duration (e.g., slow the eccentric).

**Guidance:**
- Beginners: aim for some progress every workout.
- Intermediate/advanced: think in longer time scales; improve technique, tempo, and mind-muscle connection.
- Adding weight only counts if form stays consistent — momentum, reduced ROM, or altered mechanics do not count.

---

### 1.6 Training Volume

Volume = number of hard (working) sets per session or per week. Warm-up sets don't count.

**Volume landmarks:**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| MV (Maintenance Volume) | Volume needed to maintain current physique |
| MED (Minimum Effective Dose) | Least volume to produce some growth |
| MAV (Maximum Adaptive Volume) | Sweet spot — maximizes progress without excess fatigue |
| MRV (Maximum Recoverable Volume) | Most volume recoverable from; beyond this, performance suffers |

**General principles:**
- Beginners need less volume than advanced trainees.
- Start new programs with lower volume and increase gradually.
- Most trainees thrive on **10–20 sets per muscle group per week**.
- Adding volume is not the only way to drive progress — master technique and effort first.
- Not all sets are equal: squats impose far more fatigue than leg press for the same set count.

**Volume levels:**

| Level | Hard sets per exercise | Hard sets per week (per muscle) |
|---|---|---|
| Low | 1–2 | 2–8 |
| Moderate | 2–3 | 8–15 |
| High | 4–6 | 15–30 |

---

### 1.7 Exercise Selection

**Prioritize compound (multi-joint) movements** — they build general strength and are time-efficient.

| Region | Recommended compounds |
|---|---|
| Legs | Squat, lunge, leg press, deadlift variation, hip thrust |
| Back / Biceps | Row variation (horizontal pull), pull-up / pulldown (vertical pull) |
| Chest / Triceps | Horizontal press (dumbbell, barbell, machine) |
| Shoulders | Vertical press (barbell, dumbbell) |

**Add isolation movements** for lagging muscles:

| Muscle | Isolation options |
|---|---|
| Rear delts | Reverse pec deck, reverse flyes |
| Side delts | Lateral raise (cable, dumbbell, machine) |
| Biceps | Curl variation |
| Triceps | Cable pressdown, overhead extension, skull crusher |
| Hamstrings | Leg curl variation |
| Calves | Calf raise (standing, seated) |
| Abs | Cable/weighted crunch, leg raise, plank |

Include **a variety of exercises** per muscle group — studies show different exercises grow different heads of the same muscle. Example: quad-only squats built less of the "teardrop" VMO than a program using squats, leg press, lunges, and deadlifts combined.

---

### 1.8 Effort — How Hard to Train

High effort is required for continued progress beyond the beginner stage.

- **Compound exercises:** leave ~1–3 reps in reserve (RIR) on most sets.
- **Isolation exercises:** can go to failure more routinely; reserve failure for the last set of each exercise.
- Consistently going to complete failure on every set can cause overtraining and reduce total volume capacity.

**RIR / RPE Scale:**

| RPE | RIR | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 0 | Maximum effort — failure |
| 9 | 1 | 1 rep left |
| 8 | 2 | 2 reps left |
| 7 | 3 | 3 reps left |
| 6 | 4 | 4 reps left |
| 5 | 5+ | Light effort |

**Safety note:** For barbell squats, incline press, leg press, and lunges, RIR 0 means you couldn't complete another clean rep — but you do NOT need to actually fail the rep (injury risk outweighs benefit). For all other exercises, go to genuine failure when prescribed.

---

### 1.9 Rep Ranges

Similar muscle growth can be achieved across a wide spectrum of rep ranges, but there is a **practical hypertrophy zone of 6–15 reps.**

- **Below 6 reps:** heavy loads carry higher injury risk; harder to accumulate sufficient volume.
- **Above 15 reps:** metabolic fatigue accumulates quickly; psychologically and physiologically taxing.
- **Above 15 reps** are best reserved for isolation exercises at the end of workouts.

**Recommended allocation:** ~75% of weekly volume in the 6–15 rep zone, with some <6 (strength) and some >15 (pump/endurance) work.

---

### 1.10 Training Frequency and Splits

Training each muscle **at least twice per week** produces better results than once per week (traditional bro-split). Whether higher than 2× is better depends on the individual.

**Split options:**

| Split | Frequency | Level |
|---|---|---|
| Full Body | 3 days/week | Beginner–Intermediate |
| Upper / Lower | 4 days/week | Beginner–Intermediate |
| Upper / Lower | 6 days/week | Intermediate–Advanced |
| Push / Pull / Legs | 6 days/week | Intermediate–Advanced |
| Full Body | 5 days/week | Advanced–Elite (reduce volume/intensity per session) |

---

### 1.11 Energy Systems in Training

- **Heavy weights, low reps (1–6):** primarily uses the **phosphocreatine (PCr) system** (creatine phosphate as fuel).
- **Moderate-to-high reps (6–20), high volume:** primarily uses the **glycolytic system** (burns carbohydrates).
- High-rep, high-volume sessions (e.g., hard leg day) deplete glycogen far more than low-rep strength sessions. This matters for recovery structure.

---

### 1.12 Cardio

Cardio has the potential to **interfere with muscle-building adaptations** (the interference effect). Recommendation: keep it to an **effective minimum** while prioritizing recomposition.

**Factors that increase interference:**
- Higher frequency (more sessions per week).
- Higher intensity (HIIT > LISS).
- Higher impact (running > cycling > swimming).

**LISS vs HIIT:**

| Aspect | LISS (e.g., 30-min incline walk) | HIIT (e.g., 6×20s sprints) |
|---|---|---|
| Recovery demand | Low | High |
| Interference with lifting | Low | Higher |
| Time to complete | Longer | Shorter |
| Best use | Daily energy expenditure, fat loss | Cardiovascular health, time-limited |

**Guidelines:**
- Maximum ~5 LISS sessions/week; 0–5 based on lifestyle.
- Maximum **1–2 HIIT sessions/week**.
- Prefer lower-impact modalities (cycling, swimming, walking over running on pavement).
- Never do long or intense cardio **immediately before** weight training — it depletes energy needed for productive lifting.
- Fasted cardio has no proven fat-loss advantage over fed cardio; do whichever fits your schedule.

**NEAT (Non-Exercise Activity Thermogenesis):** everyday movement matters. A daily step goal of ~8,000 steps is a practical target for sedentary trainees.

**Cardio by profile examples:**

| Profile | Cardio need | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| Sedentary office job, 35% BF | High | 30 min LISS, 5×/week @ ~60% HRmax |
| Physically active job (waitress, ~15k steps/day) | Low | Optional HIIT 1–2×/week post-workout |
| Sedentary college student, 30% BF | High | 30 min LISS, 5×/week |
| 12% BF, muscle-building goal, moderate NEAT | Low | Optional HIIT 1×/week |
| Office job, pet owner | High | Walk at lunch, stairs, walk the dog, aim for ~10k steps/day |

### 1.13 Sleep — A Critical Recovery Variable

Sleep is one of the most underappreciated components of recomposition.

**Sleep and body composition research:**
- Subjects sleeping one hour less per night (5 nights/week) in a caloric deficit lost ~85% of weight as fat-free mass (muscle), vs. normal-sleep group losing ~83% as fat — nearly opposite outcomes.
- Another study (8.5 vs. 5.5 hrs/night): both groups lost ~6.5 lb total, but the sleep-restricted group lost only ~1.3 lb of fat (~80% came from muscle). Sleep-restricted group also had elevated **ghrelin** (hunger hormone).
- Proposed mechanism: sleep restriction causes higher cortisol, lower testosterone, and lower IGF-1 — reducing muscle protein synthesis and increasing breakdown.

**Sleep and performance:**
- Restricting to 3 hours/night for 3 days significantly reduced max weights on bench, deadlift, and leg press — getting worse each day.
- Partial sleep deprivation increases **perceived effort** at a given weight.

**Sleep extension benefits:**
- Stanford swimmers extending sleep to 10 hrs improved sprint times, reaction time, and set records.
- Basketball players at 10+ hrs improved free-throw accuracy by ~9% and 3-point accuracy by ~9.2%.

**Recommendations:**
- Target **8–9 hours per night** for trainees.
- One hour of sleep debt takes ~4 days of optimal sleep to recover.
- Any improvement helps — 6 hrs better than 5, 7 better than 6.
- Naps are useful for shift workers or those with sleep disorders.

**Sleep hygiene tips:**
- Same bedtime and wake time daily.
- No intense exercise within a few hours of bed.
- Avoid caffeine after 5pm, alcohol before bed.
- Cool, dark, quiet room; no bright screens in bed.
- Wind down with a bath, reading, or relaxing routine.
- Write down worries before bed so your mind can let them go.

---

### 1.14 Stress and Recovery

In a 12-week study, subjects with higher life stress gained significantly less strength on bench and squat, and less size on arms and thighs. Stress takes a measurable toll on training recovery.

**Stress-management tools:**
- Meditation / mindfulness (even 10–15 min guided sessions).
- Flow activities: sports, music, art, video games, gardening — anything where challenge meets skill.
- Physical activity you enjoy; time with positive people.
- Daily gratitude practice; self-compassion; celebrating small wins.
- Avoid maladaptive coping (alcohol, overeating, etc.).

---

### 1.15 Progress Tracking

No single metric is sufficient. Use a combination.

**Mandatory tools:**

| Tool | How to use | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Weight scale | Weigh daily (same time, post-bathroom, pre-food), calculate weekly average, compare weekly averages | Daily weigh-in, weekly average |
| Measuring tape — waist | Measure at belly button or smallest waist site (be consistent). Waist shrinking while scale rises = recomposition happening. | 1–2× per month |
| Measuring tape — body parts | Largest site on each muscle; record both left and right for limbs. | Every 1–3 months |
| Progress photos | Same location, lighting, time of day, poses (front/back/side). Natural light, fasted, self-timer. | Weekly or monthly |

**Optional / advanced tools:**

| Tool | What it measures | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Skinfold calipers | Subcutaneous fat directly | 3-site: belly, triceps, anterior thigh. 7-site adds chest, subscap, hip, axilla. |
| BIA (bioelectrical impedance) | Fat vs. lean mass estimate | Track trends, not absolute numbers. InBody is most accurate consumer format. |
| DEXA scan | Bone, lean, and fat mass | Most accurate; ~6-minute scan; not for pregnant women. |

## PART 2 — LOW-VOLUME HIGH-INTENSITY PROGRAM (12 Weeks)

### 2.1 Overview

Low-volume, high-intensity hypertrophy program. The central premise: **effort matters more than volume**. As long as effort is high, significant gains can be made with relatively few sets.

**Designed for:**
1. Trainees who want quick but effective workouts.
2. Trainees who prefer low volume / high intensity.
3. Trainees cutting (deficit) who want to maximize muscle retention.

**Structure:**
- **12 weeks** split into two 6-week blocks.
- **5 sessions/week:** Upper 1 / Lower 1 / Upper 2 / Lower 2 / Arms + Delts.
- **~45 minutes per session.**
- **Week 1:** intro week (RIR 1–2 throughout).
- **Week 7:** deload week (RIR 1–2 throughout).
- **Weeks 2–6:** working weeks, most sets RIR 0–1.
- **Weeks 8–12:** working weeks + intensity techniques on last set of select exercises.
- Exercises, sets, and rep ranges are **identical all 12 weeks** — only effort and techniques change.

---

### 2.2 Effort System (RIR)

RIR = Reps In Reserve — how many more reps you could have done at the end of a set.

- **RIR 2** = stopped with 2 reps left.
- **RIR 1** = stopped with 1 rep left.
- **RIR 0** = true failure.

Most sets in this program go to RIR 0 or 1. Safety exception: for squat, barbell incline press, leg press, and Smith machine lunge, "RIR 0" means you couldn't do another clean rep — don't risk a failed attempt under load.

---

### 2.3 Effort Progression Across 12 Weeks

| Weeks | Block | Effort | Intensity Techniques |
|---|---|---|---|
| Week 1 | Block 1 | Intro — RIR 1–2 | None |
| Weeks 2–6 | Block 1 | RIR 0–1 | None |
| Week 7 | Block 2 | Deload — RIR 1–2 | None |
| Weeks 8–12 | Block 2 | RIR 0–1 | Added on last set of select exercises |

---

### 2.4 Intensity Techniques (Block 2, last set only)

- **Drop sets:** After failure, reduce weight ~25% and go to failure again; repeat once more (3 total back-to-back mini-sets).
- **Lengthened partials:** After failure with full ROM, continue partial reps in the stretched position until failure.
- **Myo-reps:** After failure, rest 5 sec, do 2 more reps; repeat cycle (5 sec + 2 reps) until you can't complete 2 reps (usually by 3rd cycle).
- **Weighted static hold:** After failure on machine chest press, hold the bottom (stretched) position under tension for 30 seconds.

### 2.6 Warm-Up Protocol

**General warm-up:** 5–10 min light cardio; optionally: arm circles (10/side), cable external rotations (15/side), arm swings (10/side), leg swings front-back and side-side (10/side).

**Exercise-specific warm-up sets:**
- **1 warm-up set:** ~60% working weight × 6–10 reps.
- **2 warm-up sets:** 50% × 6–10, then 70% × 4–6.
- **3 warm-up sets:** 45% × 6–10, 65% × 4–6, 85% × 3–4.
- **4 warm-up sets:** 45% × 6–10, 60% × 4–6, 75% × 3–5, 85% × 2–4.

**Rest guidelines:**
- Between warm-up sets: 0–60 sec.
- Between working sets: heavy compounds 3–5 min; lighter compounds and isolations 1–2 min.
- Between exercises: 1–5 min depending on demand.

---

### 2.7 Progression Rule

Once you can complete all working sets at the **top** of the rep range, increase load next session. If adding weight drops you below the range, keep the same weight and extend the range slightly (e.g., 6–8 prescribed → add weight drops you to 5 reps → stay at previous load, aim for 6–10 next time).

## PART 3 — PUSH/PULL/LEGS ADVANCED PROGRAM (4x/Week, 13 Weeks)

### 3.1 Overview

Hypertrophy-focused with a minimalist strength component on the three powerlifts (squat, bench, deadlift).

**Designed for:** Intermediate-to-advanced trainees (~2–5 years consistent, focused training). Beginners should build a base first.

**Split:** Legs / Push / Pull / Full Body — 4 days/week, 3 rest days.

**Frequency:** Each major muscle group trained ~2×/week.

**Total length:** 13 weeks across three phases plus a deload.

**Three sister versions:** 6×/week PPL (1 rest day, more volume), 5×/week PPL/Upper/Lower (2 rest days). If recovery is a problem, drop to the 4×/week version.

---

### 3.2 Phase Overview

| Phase | Weeks | Volume | Intensity | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 — Base Hypertrophy | 1–6 | Moderate-high | Moderate-high | Build hypertrophy foundation |
| Phase 2 — Maximum Effort | 7–10 | Low | Ultra-high | Familiarity with true failure; drive progressive overload |
| Phase 3 — Supercompensation | 11–12 | Ultra-high | Moderate-high | Sudden volume spike for new growth stimulus |
| Deload | 13 | Reduced | Reduced | Recovery |

---

### 3.3 Key Concepts

**Autoregulation / RPE:** Adjust load day-to-day based on how you feel. Choose a weight such that you hit the prescribed RPE on the **last** working set. Earlier sets will feel easier. On a strong day, push heavier; on an off day, go lighter. This produces better strength gains than fully fixed programming.

**Progression:**
- Add reps until you hit the top of the rep range.
- Once there, add weight and start again from the bottom.
- Phase 2 (4–6 reps): if you hit 6 reps with good form, add weight next session.
- Phases 1 and 3: if adding reps/weight isn't possible every week, improve technique or mind-muscle connection instead.

**Supersets (A1/A2):** Do A1, move immediately to A2 with no rest, then rest for the time listed in the A2 row before repeating.

**Cluster sets:** Many sets of low reps with very short rest (e.g., 6 × 3 reps, 15 sec rest). Used in Phase 3.

---

### 3.4 Phase 1 — Base Hypertrophy (Weeks 1–6)

Structure: 6–8 exercises/day, 2–4 working sets, mostly 10–12 reps. Compounds ~RPE 8–9; isolations ~RPE 9–10. Advanced techniques included throughout. **Week 6 is a semi-deload** (RPE 7–8, ~2 working sets per exercise).

**Main lift rep wave (top set):**

| Lift | Wk 1 | Wk 2 | Wk 3 | Wk 4 | Wk 5 | Wk 6 (semi-deload) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Squat | 2–4 | 3–5 | 4–6 | 3–5 | 2–4 | 1–3 |
| Bench Press | 3–5 | 3–5 | 2–4 | 2–4 | 3–5 | 3–5 |
| Deadlift | 5 | 4 | 3 | 2 | 1 | 4 |

## PART 5 — ADVANCED TRAINING TECHNIQUES EXPLAINED

| Technique | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Drop sets** | Hit failure → immediately reduce load ~25% → go to failure again → repeat once more (3 total mini-sets). |
| **Lengthened partials** | Hit failure with full ROM → continue partial reps in the bottom (stretched) position until failure. |
| **Myo-reps** | Hit failure → rest 5 sec → do 2 reps → repeat cycle until can't complete 2 full reps. |
| **Weighted static hold** | After failure, hold the stretched position under full load for 30 seconds. |
| **Feeder sets** | A ramp-up of progressively heavier sets leading into one true working set (e.g., 4 sets of 10 from RPE 4→6→8→10). |
| **Cluster sets** | Many low-rep sets (e.g., 6 × 3) with very short rest (15 sec) — high-quality reps with moderate loads. |
| **Supersets** | Two exercises back-to-back with no rest between; rest only after the second exercise. |
| **Loaded static stretch** | Hold a muscle in a stretched position for ~30 sec at ~7/10 intensity, often paired as a superset with a working set. |
| **Mechanical dropset** | Extend a set by changing technique (e.g., switching grip or angle) rather than reducing load. |
| **Omni-grip / omni-direction** | Use a different grip width or cable angle on each set to vary the stimulus. |
| **Bottom-half / stretch partials** | Perform only the stretched (bottom) half of ROM. |
| **Top-half / squeeze partials** | Perform only the contracted (top) half of ROM. |
| **Back-off sets** | After a heavy top set, reduce weight significantly and perform additional volume sets. |
| **AMRAP finishers** | An open-ended "as many reps as possible" set at the end of an exercise. |
| **21s** | A bicep-curl variation: 7 bottom-half reps + 7 top-half reps + 7 full-ROM reps. |
| **Acceptable cheating** | Mild, controlled body momentum on the concentric only (e.g., Kroc rows, cheat curls), while controlling the eccentric strictly. |
| **Slow tempo** | Deliberately count seconds on both concentric and eccentric to increase time under tension (e.g., 3-sec up / 3-sec down). |

## PART 7 — FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

**Q: Can I build muscle and lose fat at the same time?**
Yes — this is body recomposition. It is easiest for beginners, returning lifters, obese individuals, and steroid users, but possible at any level with the right approach. It can look like simultaneously losing fat and gaining muscle, gaining muscle with stable fat mass, or losing fat with stable muscle mass.

**Q: How many sets should I do per muscle per week?**
Most trainees thrive on 10–20 sets per muscle per week. Start closer to 10 and increase over time. The right amount is highly individual — some need more, some less. More is not always better; too much can cause overtraining.

**Q: How many reps should I do?**
For hypertrophy, 6–15 reps covers most of your volume. Below 6 = more strength-focused with higher injury risk; above 15 = more metabolic fatigue. Aim for ~75% of volume in the 6–15 zone.

**Q: How many days per week should I train?**
Training each muscle at least 2× per week is better than once. 3–6 days/week total works for most people. Pick a split that fits your schedule and allows recovery.

**Q: Should I train to failure?**
Not on every set. Compounds: leave 1–3 reps in reserve on most sets. Isolations: can go to failure on the last set. Constant failure training can cause overtraining and reduce total volume capacity.

**Q: What's better — low volume high intensity or high volume moderate intensity?**
Both can build muscle. Low volume/high intensity works well when recovering is harder (cutting, limited time). Higher volume may build muscle faster when recovery supports it. Most programs combine both approaches across phases.

**Q: How important is cardio for body recomposition?**
Cardio is supportive but not the driver — that's weight training. Keep cardio to an effective minimum. LISS (low intensity) 0–5×/week; HIIT maximum 1–2×/week. Never do long/intense cardio right before lifting.

**Q: How important is sleep?**
Extremely. Sleeping one hour less per night can flip your body composition outcomes from mostly fat loss to mostly muscle loss when in a deficit. Target 8–9 hours. Any improvement helps.

**Q: I'm not getting sore — is the program working?**
Soreness is not a reliable indicator of muscle growth. Reduced soreness over time often means your body is adapting, which is a good sign. As long as you're pushing hard and progressing, you don't need soreness.

**Q: Can I substitute exercises?**
Yes. Prioritize the prescribed movements — they're chosen for their tension profile and stimulus-to-fatigue ratio — but substitute if you can't access equipment, feel an exercise in your joints rather than the target muscle, or lack a good mind-muscle connection on a movement. Keep sets, reps, and effort identical.

**Q: Can I change the exercise order if the gym is crowded?**
Yes, within reason. Keep compound movements earlier in the workout; save isolation work for later so you're not pre-fatigued for the big lifts.

**Q: How do I know if I'm progressing?**
Track strength (weight × reps per exercise), weekly average bodyweight, waist circumference, and progress photos. Steadily increasing strength on key exercises is the best day-to-day proxy for muscle progress.

**Q: How much muscle can I gain per month?**
Highly individual. Rough ballparks: untrained ~1–2 lb/month; 1 year of training ~0.5–1 lb/month; intermediate-advanced ~0.25–0.5 lb/month. Women roughly half these rates. Progress slows as you advance.

**Q: I can only train 3 days per week. What should I do?**
Run a Full Body split (3×/week) or use the low-volume high-intensity program as a rolling 3-day cycle (carrying the remaining workout into the following week). Don't skip any of the prescribed days — do them in order.

**Q: Should I add cardio to these programs?**
Optional. If adding cardio for general health, keep it short and low-to-moderate intensity — about 20–30 min, 3–5×/week. Limit HIIT to 1–2 sessions per week since it can compromise recovery from high-intensity resistance training.

**Q: What gear is useful for lifting?**
- **Chalk or straps** — improve grip on deadlifts and rows.
- **Lifting belt (10mm prong/lever)** — for hard sets on squat, bench, deadlift, and overhead press; not for warm-ups.
- **Knee sleeves** — for squat variations and leg press.
- **Elevated-heel squat shoes** — improve depth and stability, especially with limited ankle mobility.
All optional — no gear is required to build muscle or strength.

**Q: What do I do after finishing a program?**
Run the same program again from Week 1, or move on to a different program. Cycling back through is valid — you'll use heavier weights and the training stimulus remains productive.
